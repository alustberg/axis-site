The Physics of Leadership

A single motion.

He thought he was losing his edge — the judgment, the instinct, the thing that used to make him good. The story was easy to believe: he'd risen past his depth, and now the gap was showing. It wasn't that. A trusted colleague suggested he reach out to me to navigate these doubts.

Years earlier, he'd designed how his company really ran. Not the org chart — the machinery underneath it: how a decision moved from a hallway conversation to something a couple hundred people were executing within weeks, without him in the room. He never wrote it down. It just worked, because he'd built it like a house he meant to live in. The same thing that made it good is what made it hard to hand off: you don't draft blueprints for a house you actually live in.

He was good at it, so they promoted him. And promoted him again. Each step up took him a layer further from the thing he'd made. He handed it down to people who ran it competently and a little differently, and who then handed pieces of it down again.

By the time we talked, the machine was running on a dozen other hands and drifting a few degrees from what he'd intended. It was no longer what he built, and it wasn't producing the same type of outcomes. He could see exactly what was off, but he was too far above it to reach in. That distance was the whole story.

Not lost capability — lost proximity.

The instrument was fine. He was standing too far from the thing it works on. I've noticed this is the quiet tax on being operationally gifted: do the work well enough and you get moved away from the work. The prize for building the machine is a spectator seat above it.

From the outside, that can look like stalling. So people start using the usual words: stretch, development, executive altitude, whatever they'd agreed to call the problem.

But there was nothing wrong with his judgment. The issue was that his judgment no longer had a clean line to the thing it was built to sense. You don't train your way across a gap like that. You close it.

Which doesn't mean going back. He didn't need to run the machine again. He needed to get back within reach of it, but he couldn't see this on his own. From where he sat, it just felt like decline. It took an outside read to call it distance, and to find the one place where closing the gap would matter most.

So he did — he started running the weekly operations meeting again, close enough that his read on it went live instead of remembered.

The judgment came back, because it had never left; it had just been pointed at nothing. What surprised him was the energy — all the bandwidth he'd been pouring into doubt, suddenly free.

I wonder how many senior people are in this situation — being developed for a decline that isn't happening.
